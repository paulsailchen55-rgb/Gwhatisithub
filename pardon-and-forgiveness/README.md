# Pardon and Forgiveness: Two Kinds of Release

## Status

**PROPOSED CONCEPT / PHILOSOPHICAL RESEARCH NOTE**

This document preserves a distinction developed in conversation between **pardon** and **forgiveness**. It is a conceptual framework for investigation, not a settled historical, theological, linguistic, or legal conclusion.

## Core observation

A pardon and forgiveness can both be described as forms of **release**, but the object of release can be different.

- **Pardon:** legal release from some consequence, penalty, disability, or punishment imposed by an authority.
- **Forgiveness:** release of an offense, grievance, resentment, or continuing claim from the relationship between people.

The central question is whether treating the two as interchangeable causes a legal meaning created within a particular institution and historical period to be mistaken for the whole human meaning of forgiveness.

## The perception problem

A legal institution can use a familiar moral word in a specialized technical context.

The modern federal government itself describes a pardon as an expression of the President's forgiveness, while also explaining that a pardon does not erase the conviction or establish innocence. See the U.S. Department of Justice, Office of the Pardon Attorney: https://www.justice.gov/pardon/frequently-asked-questions

This creates an important distinction:

> **The legal system can define what a pardon does without thereby defining everything that forgiveness is.**

The word "forgiveness" may therefore function as a bridge between a legal mechanism and a broader human/religious concept. The bridge is real, but the concepts need not be identical.

## A working distinction

**Pardon:** “I have authority over the consequence, and I release you from that consequence.”

**Forgiveness:** “I have a grievance or claim arising from what happened, and I release that grievance or claim from continuing to govern the relationship.”

This means:

- a person may forgive without having legal authority;
- a government may pardon without creating reconciliation, trust, or personal forgiveness;
- a pardon can change legal consequences without erasing the historical event or all social consequences;
- forgiveness can occur without changing any legal record.

## Debt and forgiveness

The biblical language is especially interesting because **debt and forgiveness are explicitly connected**.

Matthew 18 uses a debt/servant relationship to illustrate forgiveness: a master releases a servant from an enormous debt, and the servant subsequently refuses to release another person's debt.

The Lord's Prayer likewise joins the concepts of **debts** and **forgiveness**.

This should not be simplified into the claim that "forgiveness means debt forgiveness." Rather, the texts provide a conceptual bridge between **what is owed, what is claimed, what is released, and what continues to be held against another person**.

## Jesus: what continues versus what is released

Mark 9:48 invokes the image of the worm that does not die and the fire that is not quenched, drawing on Isaiah 66:24.

For this project, the important conceptual observation is not a definitive doctrine about the passage. It is the contrast between:

- something that **continues to consume**; and
- something that has been **released**.

That makes forgiveness worth examining as a process of ending the continuing grip of an offense, rather than merely as the cancellation of a legal penalty.

## A legal boundary

The U.S. Constitution expressly gives the President power to grant “Reprieves and Pardons for Offences against the United States, except in Cases of Impeachment.” Article II, Section 2.

The constitutional text says **pardon**, not "forgiveness."

The Department of Justice describes pardon as a form of executive clemency and uses the language of forgiveness in explaining it. It also states that a pardon does not expunge the conviction and does not signify innocence.

Therefore, this project should preserve the distinction rather than collapse the terms.

## The Supreme Court question

A further research question arises from Supreme Court pardon jurisprudence:

> **When a court gives a legal term a particular meaning within a particular constitutional and historical setting, how much of that institutional meaning should be treated as the meaning of the underlying human concept?**

This document deliberately does **not** call that a "trick" as an established historical fact.

The stronger research formulation is:

> **Could a specialized legal meaning create a perception in which pardon and forgiveness appear synonymous even though they operate on different objects?**

That is the question to investigate.

## Connection to punishment and social consequences

This distinction may also connect to Alexandra Natapoff's work on misdemeanor punishment.

If punishment can end while a person's conviction continues to generate housing, employment, licensing, or other consequences, then a system may provide a mechanism for **punishment release** without providing a comparable mechanism for **social release**.

That is a research question, not a conclusion.

## Working model

**Immunity** → the person is protected from a specified legal consequence.

**Pardon** → an authority releases the person from specified legal consequences.

**Forgiveness** → a person releases an offense, grievance, or continuing claim from the relationship.

**Restoration** → the relationship or person's standing is rebuilt after harm.

These concepts can overlap without being identical.

## Open questions

1. How did "forgive" and "pardon" develop historically in English?
2. When did legal institutions begin explicitly describing pardon as forgiveness?
3. How did Roman, canon, English common-law, and American legal traditions influence the terminology?
4. What exactly did early American constitutional commentators mean by "pardon"?
5. How does Supreme Court pardon doctrine distinguish legal effect from historical guilt?
6. Can a legal system create a genuine mechanism for completion/release without requiring the person to remain permanently defined by the offense?
7. How does this relate to misdemeanor records and collateral consequences?
8. Can forgiveness be represented institutionally without turning it into another form of punishment or authority?
