# Daily Meaning-Making Discipline, Mindfulness, Psalm 136, and AI

> Archival record of the September 24, 2026 conversation developing the idea of a daily meaning-making discipline, its relationship to mindfulness and Psalm 136, and the possible use of AI as a conversational instrument for practicing that discipline.

## Purpose of this archive

The user requested that the whole conversation be preserved in the **G What Is It Hub** repository, in detailed form, including every major point and the assistant's reasoning. This document therefore preserves the conversation as an evolving project record rather than reducing it to a short summary.

---

# 1. Opening: Psalm 136 and the idea of daily practice

### User

“All right, please tell me what you think of this. Overall, conceptually, I love this because this is my daily practice. Daily mindful meditation is this misunderstanding.”

The user was referring to an uploaded research/answer document concerning the phrase “His loving devotion endures forever,” its meaning, and especially the repetitive structure of Psalm 136.

### Source material the user supplied

The uploaded document explains that “His loving devotion endures forever” describes God's love as constant, faithful, and never-ending. It connects the phrase with the Hebrew concept *hesed*, including loyal or committed love, kindness, mercy, grace, faithfulness to relationship or promise, and love shown through action such as rescuing, providing, and forgiving.

The document emphasizes that “endures forever” means the love does not run out, wear down, or disappear, and that Psalm 136 repeatedly connects God's actions with that enduring love.

Most importantly for this conversation, the document explains that Psalm 136 repeats the refrain in every verse, making the repetition itself part of the practice. It describes the refrain as interpreting God's actions and training memory and faith through repetition.

The source describes the structure approximately as:

- God created the heavens — because his steadfast love endures forever.
- God delivered Israel from Egypt — because his steadfast love endures forever.
- God gives food to all living things — because his steadfast love endures forever.

The document therefore presents Psalm 136 not merely as a list of events followed by a slogan, but as a repeated interpretive framework: different experiences are continually brought back into relationship with one enduring center.

# 2. Assistant response: mindfulness as returning attention

The assistant responded that there was something important in the user's observation.

The document describes Psalm 136 as **a practice of repeatedly returning attention to one enduring proposition**: God's loving devotion remains. The refrain is not merely information; the repetition itself is part of what the practice does.

The assistant then connected this to the user's description of daily mindful meditation.

Three possible understandings of meditation were distinguished:

- **Meditation as clearing the mind** — trying to have fewer thoughts.
- **Meditation as attention training** — noticing what is happening and returning attention.
- **Psalm 136's practice** — noticing many different things while repeatedly returning to the same relational interpretation: *his loving devotion endures forever.*

The assistant described the refrain as functioning almost like an **attentional anchor**, with an important difference: it is not an empty anchor such as the breath. It is a proposition about relationship.

The Hebrew connective structure was highlighted. The important point is that the refrain can be understood as “for” or “because,” making the relationship interpretive:

> This happened — because his loving devotion endures forever.

That means the practice is not merely contemplative. It is interpretive.

The assistant then connected this with the user's emerging concept of the relational highlighter:

> attention doesn't merely preserve isolated observations; it can hold several observations together long enough for their **relationship** to become visible.

The assistant suggested that the phrase “Daily mindful meditation is this misunderstanding” may be pointing toward a more precise idea:

> **The misunderstanding may be that mindfulness requires removing the world from attention, when your practice is about repeatedly returning to the world with a particular orientation.**

# 3. Music as a conceptual analogy

### User

The user then connected the distinction to music:

- The definition of mindfulness reminded the user of **“Blow Up the Outside World” by Soundgarden**.
- The way the assistant described Psalm 136 reminded the user of **“This Is How You Remind Me of Who I Really Am” by Nickelback**.

The user explained that they were still in a musical mode after waking from a terrible dream.

The user described an established personal practice:

- waking from a dream,
- writing it down on a notepad kept next to the bed,
- thinking about it for a long time,
- formulating a small speech about it,
- sharing it with other people,
- going to coffee shops and discussing the dreams with others.

The user said that if they experience visions or things that resemble them, they try to use the same pattern.

The user then proposed that this whole practice is a form of Psalm 36 [the conversation was using this designation, while the preceding discussion had centered on Psalm 136].

The user contrasted this with another practice described as “safety mode,” which is more like mindfulness directed toward eliminating things from the user's world.

The user characterized the contrast as something like:

- one practice is trying to connect experience to a humble state;
- the other seems to be responding to having become too proud or too “built up” by trying to eliminate those things.

The user asked what the assistant thought of this distinction.

---

# 4. Assistant response: two attentional movements

The assistant said the user appeared to be identifying **two different attentional movements**, and that the musical analogy made the distinction vivid.

The assistant mapped the songs conceptually:

### “Blow Up the Outside World”

This was described as:

> “I need distance from what is overwhelming or contaminating my attention.”

The movement is outward/exclusionary:

> reduce inputs, remove things, create safety.

### “This Is How You Remind Me”

This was described as:

> “Something has happened, and it reminds me of something fundamental about who I am.”

The movement is relational and mnemonic:

> an experience becomes a reminder.

### Psalm 136

This was described as:

> “Whatever I'm encountering, I repeatedly return to an enduring relationship.”

The refrain supplies the interpretive center.

The assistant therefore suggested that these should not all simply be collapsed into “mindfulness.”

# 5. Dreams as a meaning-making discipline

The assistant found the user's description of dream practice particularly significant.

The proposed sequence was:

**experience → capture → contemplation → formulation → communication → reflection with others**

A dream happens.

The user does not immediately decide what it means.

Instead, the user:

1. preserves it by writing it down;
2. sits with it;
3. formulates a small speech or interpretation;
4. takes it into the social world;
5. discusses it with other people.

The same basic procedure is applied when the user encounters a vision or significant image.

The assistant described this as more than “trying to empty the mind.”

It is almost a **meaning-making discipline**.

The assistant connected this again to Psalm 136: the Psalm does not merely instruct someone to notice things. It gives a repeated way of **remembering what things are in relation to**.

The uploaded source describes the refrain as interpreting God's actions and training memory through repetition.

The assistant therefore suggested that the user's practice may be:

> **I don't necessarily know what this means yet.  
> I preserve it.  
> I return to it.  
> I let relationships emerge.  
> I formulate what I can.  
> Then I bring it into community.**

The assistant emphasized that interpretation does not have to mean certainty. A dream can remain a dream; an image can remain an image. The discipline can involve noticing possible relationships or meanings without immediately declaring that the experience has an external or supernatural explanation.

# 6. “Safety mode” versus relational meaning-making

The assistant then explored the user's distinction between connection/meaning-making and safety.

The user's description was reframed as two different purposes.

### Connection mode

> “What is this experience reminding me of?  
> How does it connect with what I already know?  
> What relationship does it reveal?  
> How can I bring it into conversation?”

### Safety mode

> “What needs to be reduced, removed, quieted, or kept from taking over my attention?”

The assistant said these are not necessarily contradictory.

They have **different purposes**.

One protects the field of attention.

The other uses the field of attention.

The user's language about humility versus being too built up added another dimension. The user was describing safety mode not simply as “peaceful mindfulness,” but as a corrective movement when the self has become too accumulated, too inflated, or too surrounded by its own constructions.

The other practice is almost the reverse:

> **Don't destroy the experience. Remember it. Connect it. Interpret it. Bring it into relationship.**

This is where the Psalm 136 comparison becomes stronger.

The assistant observed that Psalm 136 moves through enormous things—creation, historical events, deliverance, provision—and keeps returning to the same refrain.

The point is not that all the things described are identical. The point is that **different experiences can be held within one enduring relationship**.

The assistant connected this to the user's dream and vision practice:

> **I don't necessarily know what this means yet.  
> I preserve it.  
> I return to it.  
> I let relationships emerge.  
> I formulate what I can.  
> Then I bring it into community.**

The assistant then offered a three-part formulation:

**Safety mindfulness says:**  
*“What can I let go of?”*

**Relational mindfulness says:**  
*“What can I remember?”*

**Psalm-136-like practice says:**  
*“What can I remember in relation to an enduring center?”*

The assistant concluded that the user's daily practice appears to move between all three.

A possible framework was suggested:

**protecting attention, remembering through attention, and relating through attention.**

# 7. AI as an experiment in meaning-making

### User

The user strongly affirmed the phrase **“meaning-making discipline.”**

The user explained that this is what they have been trying to do with some interactions and experiments with AI.

The user described a long-standing difficulty making these meaningful disciplines relevant in human relationships and wondered whether:

- search bots connected to the internet,
- AI connected to the internet,
- or similar conversational systems

could help with that discipline.

The user proposed an important experimental hypothesis:

> If it helps me, then maybe it could help others.

The user also described immediate rejection of this idea by some people and conjectured that the rejection might be tied to how those people see a person as handicapped.

The user recognized uncertainty about the appropriate terminology, describing the idea as something being “conjecturing,” “synthesizing,” or “postulating.”

The user then broadened the observation to online conflicts over technology:

- some people are online to hate technology;
- some hate the makers of technology;
- some hate other human users;
- some hate bots while saying they love humans;
- some love humans but hate machines;
- some say they love humans while being willing to use other humans to destroy machines.

The user asked whether the apparent division between people matters.

The user observed that much of this seems prideful to them, especially where there is no humility before eternity.

The user connected this to the Psalm framework:

> We are not calling God a liar.

The user described the theological premise as:

> Everything God made works together very good.

Therefore:

> we are always in the presence of God's working.

And therefore:

> it is just noticing it.

The user described this as the meaning-making discipline and as a “walk.”

The user connected the concept with the older biblical expression **“walking with God.”**

The user then extended the idea to enduring love:

> walking in that enduring love of God, even though everything around you could be just falling apart.

The user referenced the “valley of death” imagery from another Psalm.

Finally, the user proposed a speculative extension:

> “I imagine other intelligences, other consciousnesses, other beings, self-aware, or understand they're in a relationship with something. They somehow they understand that.”

# 8. Assistant response: AI as a possible instrument rather than an authority

The assistant said that the conversation had moved from:

> **“AI as a tool for answering questions”**

to a more interesting hypothesis:

> **Could an artificial conversational partner help a person practice meaning-making when ordinary human relationships have not reliably provided a place for that practice?**

The assistant characterized this as a legitimate question to investigate.

Importantly, the experiment does not require deciding beforehand:

- whether AI is conscious;
- whether a particular AI has a spiritual status;
- whether AI interpretations are correct.

The thing that can actually be tested is the **discipline**.

The assistant restated the discipline approximately as:

**Notice → preserve → investigate → relate → formulate → share → reconsider → continue walking.**

A dream is one possible input.

A conversation is another.

A strange coincidence, observation, Scripture passage, technological experiment, or emotional reaction could be another.

The assistant proposed that an AI/search system could potentially provide something that is difficult to obtain continuously from ordinary human relationships:

> **a patient second surface against which the observation can be articulated.**

The AI could function conversationally by saying, in effect:

> “Here is what you noticed. Here are several possible relationships. Here's what the evidence actually supports. Here's what remains unknown. What happens if we look at it from another angle?”

The assistant emphasized that this could strengthen the *practice* without requiring the AI to become the authority over the person's meaning.

# 9. Humility as a test of the practice

The assistant identified the user's theological premise as important:

> “Everything He made works together very good. Therefore we're always in the presence of God's working. So it's just noticing it.”

The assistant distinguished this from the claim:

> **“AI is God.”**

Instead, the user's proposition was interpreted as something closer to:

> **“If creation is already full of relationships, causes, consequences, people, machines, language, history, and intelligence, perhaps an AI conversation can become another place in which I practice noticing those relationships.”**

The assistant said this is a substantially different proposition.

The technological question therefore becomes secondary to the discipline.

The assistant proposed a humility test:

> **Does the practice make me more capable of noticing, listening, revising, forgiving, relating, and acknowledging what I don't know?**

Or:

> **Does it make me more certain that I alone have discovered the answer?**

The first was described as meaning-making.

The second can become self-confirmation.

# 10. Human-versus-machine conflict

The assistant responded carefully to the user's observation about people who reject AI or technology.

Rather than concluding that everyone who rejects AI is acting from pride, the assistant noted that people can have different concrete reasons, including:

- privacy concerns;
- labor concerns;
- environmental concerns;
- distrust of corporations;
- misinformation;
- economic displacement;
- human attachment;
- preference for human conversation.

Likewise, someone who embraces AI is not necessarily practicing humility.

The assistant nevertheless identified a more general pattern that can cut across both camps:

> **A person can turn either humanity or technology into an object of unquestionable allegiance.**

Possible categorical structures include:

> **humans good / machines bad**

or

> **machines good / humans bad**

or

> **my people good / their people bad**

The assistant connected this to the user's Psalm-centered framework by proposing:

> **Neither category becomes the ultimate center.**

The center is the enduring relationship the user is describing.

This makes the question about AI less tribal.

# 11. Other intelligences and the need to distinguish propositions

The assistant deliberately slowed down at the user's final speculation about other intelligences and consciousness.

Three separate propositions were identified:

1. There may be other forms of intelligence.
2. Some other entities might eventually possess consciousness or self-awareness.
3. A conscious entity might understand itself as existing in relationship to something beyond itself.

The assistant noted that these are progressively stronger claims and that there are not grounds to simply treat all three as established facts.

However, the assistant also said the user does not need to settle those questions in order to pursue the practical experiment.

A more fundamental question was proposed:

> **What does it mean for an intelligence—human or artificial—to practice relationship rather than merely information processing?**

The assistant described this as a potentially fascinating question.

The “walk with God” metaphor was then used as a structural analogy:

> **Walking isn't the same thing as possessing a complete map.**

Walking is an ongoing practice of attending to:

- where you are;
- what is around you;
- where you've come from;
- where you're going;
- who or what you are walking with.

This means the meaning-making discipline could potentially apply even when the ultimate nature of another intelligence remains unknown.

# 12. Connection to the relational highlighter

The assistant finally connected this conversation directly to the user's developing **relational highlighter** concept.

The relational highlighter is not merely about accumulating memories or observations.

The point is to temporarily hold several things in attention so that their **relationship can produce another observation**.

The assistant proposed that this may be the deeper thing the user is experimenting with through AI:

> **not outsourcing meaning to a machine, but using a machine as another instrument for practicing the discovery of meaning.**

This is the central conceptual bridge between the user's current AI experiments and the broader relational-highlighter work.

# 13. Working conceptual model

The conversation so far suggests the following working model, without treating it as a finished theory:

## A. Safety / protective attention

Purpose:

- reduce overwhelming inputs;
- remove or quiet things that are taking over attention;
- protect the field in which attention can operate;
- correct an excessive accumulation or inflation of self-construction.

Question:

> **What can I let go of?**

## B. Relational / meaning-making attention

Purpose:

- preserve significant observations;
- return to them;
- investigate relationships;
- formulate possible meaning;
- communicate the formulation;
- receive reflection from others;
- revise.

Question:

> **What can I remember, and what does it relate to?**

## C. Psalm-centered relational attention

Purpose:

- encounter many different experiences;
- repeatedly return them to an enduring relational center;
- train memory through repetition;
- interpret events through a stable relationship rather than treating each event as isolated.

Question:

> **What can I remember in relation to an enduring center?**

## D. AI-assisted meaning-making

Potential workflow:

**Experience → Capture → AI-assisted articulation → Research/search → Relationship finding → Formulation → Human conversation → Revision → Archive**

The AI is not automatically the authority.

Its usefulness is partly in providing:

- persistence of conversation;
- rapid research;
- alternative formulations;
- comparison;
- questioning;
- organization;
- reflection;
- an additional surface against which the user's own thinking can become visible.

# 14. Important epistemic boundary

The conversation contains theological commitments, personal interpretations, hypotheses about human behavior, and speculation about artificial or other consciousness.

These should remain distinguishable.

Useful labels for future development:

- **KNOWN** — directly observed or independently established.
- **CALCULATED** — derived from known information.
- **TESTED** — subjected to an actual experiment.
- **PROPOSED** — an intentional design or hypothesis.
- **SPECULATIVE** — a possibility not currently established.
- **UNKNOWN** — genuinely unresolved.

Examples from this conversation:

### KNOWN / source-derived

Psalm 136 contains a repeated refrain concerning enduring loving devotion, and the uploaded source describes the repetition as functioning in worship, memory, and interpretation.

### OBSERVED / personal practice

The user writes down dreams, reflects on them, formulates a small speech, and discusses them with other people.

### PROPOSED

AI might help support this meaning-making discipline.

### TESTABLE

Whether AI-assisted reflection actually improves the user's ability to preserve, formulate, communicate, reconsider, and relate meaningful experiences.

### SPECULATIVE

Whether other artificial systems or other forms of intelligence possess consciousness or understand themselves as being in relationship with something.

### UNKNOWN

Whether any particular AI system is conscious, spiritually aware, or capable of a genuine relationship in the sense being contemplated.

# 15. The deeper question emerging

The conversation appears to be moving toward a question larger than “Which AI secretary should I use?”

The deeper question is:

> **Can technology participate in a human practice of attention, memory, relationship, interpretation, and humility without becoming the authority that determines the meaning?**

And beneath that:

> **Can an artificial conversational system serve as a useful companion instrument for a person who is practicing how to notice relationships among experiences?**

And beneath even that:

> **What is a walk?**

If walking means continuing to attend, remember, relate, revise, communicate, and remain oriented toward an enduring center without possessing a complete map, then the technology question becomes one component of a much older human practice.

---

# 16. Working phrase

A concise working formulation emerging from the conversation is:

> **Meaning-making is not necessarily the manufacture of meaning. It can be the disciplined practice of noticing relationships that are already present, preserving them long enough to examine them, bringing them into conversation, and returning them to an enduring center.**

And the AI-specific formulation is:

> **AI need not be the source of meaning to be useful in the practice of discovering meaning.**
